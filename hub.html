System Overview

An operating nuclear power reactor is fundamentally designed to produce heat for electricity generation. By splitting the nuclei of heavy atoms—typically Uranium-235—in a controlled process called nuclear fission, these facilities generate vast amounts of thermal energy. This heat is transferred to a coolant fluid, which ultimately drives a steam turbine attached to an electrical generator. This basic thermodynamic framework operates similarly to fossil-fuel plants, but with virtually zero greenhouse gas emissions.

Essential Components

1. The Reactor Core

Contains all the nuclear fuel assemblies and generates 100% of the heat. It holds enriched ceramic uranium pellets housed in zirconium alloy cladding tubes, surrounded by structural components.

2. The Coolant Fluid

The medium that continuously passes through the core to absorb heat from the fuel and transfer it out to generate steam. Common coolants include light water, heavy water, or liquid metals.

3. Control Rods

Made of neutron-absorbing materials (like boron or cadmium), these rods are inserted or withdrawn from the core to precisely regulate or safely halt the rate of the fission chain reaction.

4. Containment Structure

A thick, high-density, steel-reinforced concrete dome that completely encloses the reactor pressure vessel. It acts as a vital safety barrier to isolate radiation from the environment.

Popular Reactor Designs

While various coolants and moderators exist, light-water reactors represent the vast majority of the world's functional fleet.


Pressurized Water Reactor (PWR)

The most widely deployed configuration globally. It keeps the primary water coolant under extreme pressure so that it absorbs core heat without boiling. This ultra-hot water flows to an external secondary circuit via a steam generator, where it boils a separate loop of water to spin the turbines.

Boiling Water Reactor (BWR)

The second most common design. Unlike a PWR, the primary water coolant inside a BWR is allowed to boil directly within the reactor core pressure vessel. The resulting steam is isolated, routed straight to the turbine generators, and then condensed back into liquid water.
